The Steady Body

In one day you can go from a cold AC classroom to the midday sun , from a big plate of rice to a long morning with no food , and from a full bottle of water to a football match with none . Through all of that, your body keeps three things inside you almost exactly the same: your temperature , the sugar in your blood, and the water in your blood.

Keeping conditions inside the body steady while the outside keeps changing is called homeostasis. The word comes from two Greek parts: homeo means "the same" and stasis means "standing still".

The big idea

The AC on the wall already does homeostasis

Think about the split-unit AC high up on the classroom wall. Somebody sets it on the remote, maybe to 22°C. The unit hums and blows cold air. After a while the humming stops and the room goes quiet. A few minutes later, as heat creeps back in from outside, the hum starts up again. On, off, on, off, all day long.

The AC is not guessing. Inside the unit there is a sensor that measures the temperature of the room air. When the room gets warmer than the setting, the AC switches its cooling on. When the room gets cooler than the setting, it switches the cooling off.

The same loop, inside you

Every homeostasis system in your body works like that AC. Something changes. Something detects the change. Something decides what to do. Something acts. The action pushes the change back the other way.

  1. ChangeThe room (or your body) gets warmer than the set point.
  2. DetectorA sensor notices. In the body these sensors are called receptors.
  3. Control centreSomething decides what to do. In the body this is often the hypothalamus in the brain.
  4. EffectorSomething carries out the action: the AC's cooling, or your sweat glands.

The action reverses the change, so the level comes back towards normal. Then the loop waits for the next change.

This is called negative feedback. "Negative" does not mean bad, and it does not mean cold. It means the response goes in the opposite direction to the change. Too warm, so cool down. Too cool, so warm up. Too much sugar, so take sugar out of the blood. Too little water, so hold on to water.

Section 1 of 3

Body temperature

The chemical reactions in your cells are controlled by enzymes, and your enzymes work best at about 37°C. If you get too cold, the reactions slow down. If you get too hot, the enzymes lose their shape (we say they are denatured) and stop working. That is why your body works so hard to stay close to 37°C.

Picture it

Fry an egg. The clear part turns white and solid, and no matter what you do, it never turns clear again. The heat has changed the shape of the proteins in the egg for good. Enzymes are proteins too. If your body gets too hot, they start to lose their shape in the same way, and an enzyme that has lost its shape cannot do its job.

Why each response works

Blood vessels: wider or narrower

The blood vessels do not move up or down in the skin. What changes is the width of the small arteries, called arterioles, that feed the capillaries near the surface. When they widen (vasodilation), more warm blood flows close to the air and heat radiates away. That is why your face goes red after football. When they narrow (vasoconstriction), less blood reaches the surface, so the heat stays deep inside you. That is why your fingers look pale in a cold AC room.

Sweat: it cools only when it evaporates

Sweat is mostly water, and it comes out at body temperature, so it is not cold. It cools you because water needs heat energy to change from liquid into vapour, and it takes that heat from your skin. Sweat that just drips off you has not taken much heat with it.

Try it: rub a little hand sanitiser on the back of your hand. It feels cold as it dries, because the liquid takes heat from your skin to evaporate. Sweat does the same thing, only more slowly.

Why a humid day feels hotter

When the air is already full of water vapour, sweat evaporates slowly. You stay wet, and you stay hot. That is why a still, humid afternoon in Trinidad feels worse than a dry, breezy day at the same temperature. Try it in the simulator: keep the same temperature and change only the air.

Hairs standing up

A tiny muscle, the hair erector muscle, is attached to each hair. When it contracts, it pulls the hair upright. In furry animals this traps a thick layer of still air next to the skin, and still air does not let heat pass through easily, so it works like a blanket. Think of a cat or a dog fluffing up its fur. Human body hair is too thin to trap much air, so in us it mostly shows up as goosebumps.

Shivering

Shivering is your muscles contracting and relaxing very quickly. Muscles need energy from respiration to contract, and respiration always releases some heat. It is like rubbing your hands together fast on a cold morning, except your brain does it for you with the muscles all over your body. You cannot stop shivering just by trying.

What you do also counts

The hypothalamus also makes you feel hot or cold, and that changes what you do. You move into the shade, fan yourself with your copybook, drink something cold, or put on a sweater in a cold AC room. These are part of temperature control too.

    Section 2 of 3

    Blood sugar

    Every cell in your body uses glucose in respiration to release energy. Your brain uses glucose all the time, even while you sleep. So the glucose in your blood must never fall too low. It must not stay too high either, and by the end of this section you will be able to explain why.

    A healthy level is about 4 to 7 mmol/L. You do not need to learn the unit. Just watch the number.

    Picture it

    Respiration in your cells is like the burner on a gas stove. Fuel (glucose) reacts with oxygen and energy is released, and some of it comes off as heat. Your cells do it slowly, in small controlled steps, with no flame. But the idea is the same: no fuel, no energy. That is why your blood must always carry some glucose.

    Two hormones from the pancreas

    Small groups of cells in the pancreas, called the islets of Langerhans, check the glucose level of the blood flowing past them. They are the detectors and the control centre at the same time.

    Glucose too high → insulin

    The pancreas releases insulin into the blood. Insulin lets body cells take in glucose, and it tells the liver and muscles to join spare glucose together into a storage substance called glycogen. Blood glucose falls.

    Glucose too low → glucagon

    The pancreas releases glucagon. Glucagon tells the liver to break glycogen back down into glucose and release it into the blood. Blood glucose rises. A way to remember it: glucagon is released when the glucose is gone.

    Picture it

    Think of each glucose molecule as one bead. Glycogen is hundreds of those beads strung together on long, branching strings. Strung together, they pack away neatly in the liver, like goods in a storeroom. When the blood runs short, glucagon tells the liver to snip beads off the strings and send them back out into the blood one by one.

    Insulin is a key: what goes wrong in diabetes

    This animation plays slowly and repeats. Choose Healthy, Type 1 or Type 2 to compare them.

    Type 1 diabetes

    The pancreas makes little or no insulin. It usually starts in childhood or the teenage years. With no insulin, glucose cannot get into the cells or be stored, so it stays in the blood. People with Type 1 diabetes must inject insulin every day.

    Type 2 diabetes

    The pancreas still makes insulin, but the body's cells stop responding to it properly, so glucose leaves the blood slowly. It is linked with being overweight, getting little exercise, and eating a lot of sugar and white flour or white rice. It is very common in the Caribbean. Diet, exercise and medicine help to control it.

    Reasoning it out

    What does too much sugar in the blood actually do?

    You might picture sugary blood as thick and sticky, like syrup. Let's check that with numbers. Syrup is more than half sugar: one litre of it holds over 600 g. In someone with badly controlled diabetes, one litre of blood holds about 3 or 4 g of glucose. That is nowhere near syrup. The blood still flows like blood.

    So the damage must come from something else. Sugar does two other things, and once you understand them, you can work out every problem diabetes causes for yourself.

    1. Sugar sticks to proteins

    Glucose slowly attaches itself to proteins: proteins in the blood, and proteins in the walls of blood vessels. Once it is stuck, it does not come off. You have seen this kind of chemistry before. It is the same kind of reaction that turns the outside of bread brown in the oven: sugar joining onto protein. Proteins coated with sugar become stiff and stop working properly. The lining of the vessel gets damaged, the wall thickens, and the space inside the vessel gets narrower.

    2. Sugar pulls water

    Sugar dissolved in a liquid draws water towards it by osmosis. When blood glucose goes above about 10 mmol/L, the kidneys cannot take all of it back into the blood (you will see why in Section 3). The sugar stays in the urine and holds water with it. The person passes a lot of urine, loses water, and is always thirsty. Now the blood really does become more concentrated. The full name, diabetes mellitus, comes from Greek and Latin words meaning "passing through" and "sweet as honey", because doctors long ago noticed that the urine was sweet.

    3 mo

    Doctors can measure the sticking directly. A blood test called HbA1c measures how much sugar is stuck to the haemoglobin in your red blood cells. Red blood cells live about three months, so the test shows your average blood sugar over the last three months, not just today.

        Section 3 of 3

        Water balance

        Your blood has to stay at the right concentration: not too watery, and not too salty. Every day you take in water from drinks, food and respiration, and you lose water in urine, sweat, your breath and your faeces. Your kidneys keep the balance.

        Hold on to this from the start: the kidneys cannot make water. They can only decide how much of it to keep.

        Why concentration matters

        Water moves by osmosis: it moves towards the side where there is more dissolved stuff (more salt or sugar). So the concentration of your blood decides whether water moves into your cells or out of them.

        Picture it

        Make cucumber or green mango chow. Sprinkle the salt and leave it for a few minutes, and juice collects in the bottom of the bowl. Where did that juice come from? The salt outside the cells pulled water out of them. That is osmosis. Your red blood cells would do the same thing in blood that is too salty.

        Meet the kidneys

        You have two kidneys at the back of your abdomen, just below the ribs, one on each side of the spine. Blood comes in through the renal artery, gets cleaned and balanced, and leaves through the renal vein. The urine the kidneys make flows down two tubes called ureters into the bladder, which stores it until you pass it out through the urethra.

        1 M

        Each kidney contains about one million tiny tubes called nephrons. A nephron is the working unit of the kidney. If you understand one nephron, you understand the whole kidney.

        180 L

        Your kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid out of your blood every day, almost a full blue water drum. Yet you pass only about 1.5 litres of urine. So more than 99% of what is filtered goes back into the blood. Keep that in mind as you watch the nephron.

        Step 1 up close: the filter

        The first step in the nephron is a filter. Blood flows through a knot of tiny capillaries called the glomerulus, which sits inside a cup called the Bowman's capsule. The walls of these capillaries have tiny holes in them. The blood is under high pressure, so anything small enough is pushed out through the holes into the capsule. The liquid that comes through is called the filtrate.

        Picture it

        Think of making coconut milk for callaloo or pelau. You put the grated coconut in a cloth and squeeze. The liquid goes through the cloth and the coarse bits stay behind. The squeezing is the pressure, and the cloth is the filter. The glomerulus works the same way. This is why it is called ultrafiltration: filtering under pressure, through holes so small that they sort out molecules.

        The holes are bigger than water, glucose, salt and urea, but smaller than blood cells and proteins. So the filtrate is like blood with the cells and proteins taken out. Notice that useful glucose gets through too. The nephron has to take it back later.

        1. Ultrafiltration in the glomerulus. Blood arrives in a wide arteriole and leaves through a narrower one, so the blood in the glomerulus is squeezed under high pressure, like putting your thumb over the end of a hose. Small molecules are pushed out into the Bowman's capsule. Blood cells and proteins are too big, so they stay in the blood.
        2. Selective reabsorption in the tubule. As the filtrate flows along the tubule, the useful substances are taken back into the capillaries wrapped around it: all of the glucose, most of the salt and most of the water. Most of the urea is left behind. "Selective" means the tubule chooses what to take back.
        3. Water control in the collecting duct. The final amount of water is decided here. ADH makes the duct walls let water through, so more water goes back to the blood and the urine is small and concentrated. Without ADH, the walls stay watertight and the water flows on as a lot of dilute urine.
        Picture it

        Why filter out so much, only to take most of it back? Imagine emptying your whole school bag onto the desk, then packing back everything you need and leaving the old paper and sweetie wrappers behind. It is a safe way to clean out: anything that is not deliberately packed back leaves. The nephron empties out everything small, then takes back only what the body needs.

        Why glucose can end up in the urine

        The tubule takes glucose back using carrier proteins in its walls. Think of them as a team of workers who can only carry so many bags a minute. At normal blood sugar, they carry back every single bag. When blood glucose is above about 10 mmol/L, there is more glucose in the filtrate than the workers can carry, and the extra stays in the urine. Because sugar pulls water, it holds water in the tubule too. Run the nephron explorer with very high glucose and count the water in the urine.

        ADH: the water-saving hormone

        Receptors in the hypothalamus check the concentration of the blood flowing through the brain. When the blood is too concentrated, the pituitary gland just below it releases more ADH (antidiuretic hormone) into the blood. "Diuresis" means making a lot of urine, so antidiuretic means "against making a lot of urine". ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys and opens up the collecting ducts so more water goes back into the blood. When the blood is too dilute, less ADH is released, so more water passes out as urine. The colour of your urine is a free test of this system.
